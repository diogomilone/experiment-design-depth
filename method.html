Experiment design: which choices change the call

Counting orders as independent added 17 false alarms per 100 random splits of one store over four weeks.

Over twelve weeks it added 34. Data, method, the predictions and how they came out, the changes made after the results, the checks, a glossary and the downloads.

What each part does

  • Daily checks. For each of the 381 tests, a two-sided Welch test on the running means at the first snapshot of each day (a Welch-style z test, normal approximation). The daily check calls at the first p under 0.05. The sequential rule is a mixture sequential probability ratio test that calls when the ratio reaches 20, with the mixing scale fixed at 0.01 standard deviations. It plugs in the observed variances and standard deviation at each look, a large-sample approximation of a rule whose guarantee assumes both known; its false alarms are measured by the replay. In a change added after the results, a third rule calls at a fixed bar as strict as the sequential rule (a z of at least 3.33, matched on its number of calls). The yardstick is the same Welch test at the last snapshot.
  • Relabelling replay. One seeded arrival order, 14 equal days, 2,000 relabellings. Each user is A or B with even odds; done exactly per day by a binomial draw of the arm size and a hypergeometric draw of its visits. A test checks this against explicit relabelling.
  • Per-order metrics. Orders are invoices, cancellations dropped, four weeks from 3 to 30 October 2011 and twelve weeks from 8 August. The order-level test is a Welch test on order values; the delta method uses customer sums.
  • Adjustment. Separate straight-line fits per arm, centred on the pooled mean of the features. The binned version predicts each user from 20 bins per feature, fitted on the other half of the data.
  • Segments. Cuts at the 20th to 80th percentiles; each segment against the rest, difference of the arm differences; Holm and Benjamini-Hochberg at 5%.

How much each choice moves, per 100 decisions: checking daily at the usual bar left 30.7 calls per 100 real readings that the last-snapshot test did not confirm, 23.6 more than the sequential rule (compared like for like, added after the results); counting orders as independent added 16.8 false alarms per 100 no-effect splits at four weeks and 34 at twelve; uncorrected segments, 0.1. On the no-effect replay, daily checks at the usual bar raised about 20 more false alarms per 100 splits than the sequential rule (20.5% against 0.6%).

Changes made after the first results

  • Series without variances. 15 arm and metric series carry no variances in the records. The first version counted them as tests with no call (396 tests); the design said to drop such series, and they are now dropped (381 tests). No call changed. Per 100 tests, calls that did not hold moved from 20.2 to 21.0 for the daily check and from 5.6 to 5.8 for the sequential rule; the money figures moved with them.
  • The daily look. It was first taken only at whole-day snapshots, as the design said, which the 96 series recorded at half-day offsets never reach. It is now the first snapshot of each day, so every series is read daily. This also added looks to 140 of the other 285 series, which skip a day or start at a half day, and changed a call or its day on 31. The daily check's calls went from 158, 80 not holding, to 219 and 117; the sequential rule's from 71 and 22 to 91 and 27. The gap in shares stays clear of zero (14 to 33 points), the sequential rule is lower on all four metrics, and the gap counting only calls before the last snapshot also excludes zero.
  • A daily check as strict. A third rule on the same looks: the usual test at a fixed bar matched to the sequential rule's number of calls. Its share of calls that did not hold was predicted to be below the daily check's (held: 31% against 53%) and above the sequential rule's by 5 points or more (missed: 30%, difference −2 to 6 points). So the real-test gap is credited to strictness.
  • By metric and before the last snapshot. The sequential rule's calls on each metric, and the shares counting only calls before the last snapshot, with their own interval.
  • New outputs from the same draws. The sequential rule's false alarms by look, and the combined rule with its end test at 4.5%. Every earlier replay number is unchanged.
  • New checks. An interval for the share of calls that did not hold, resampling whole experiments, and a unit test of the sequential rule with the variance estimated at each look.
  • Stopping and the per-order unit, like for like. Stopping's difference from the sequential rule, beside the per-order metric's extra false alarms, as given above.

Checks

  • Unit tests: the adjustment matches a least-squares fit, the delta method equals the simple interval when each unit has one event and is wider when events cluster, the sequential rule holds its error rate under daily looks with the variance known and with it estimated at each look, and the replay shortcut matches explicit relabelling.
  • The split between arms: Criteo against its stated share, the e-mail test against equal thirds, each retailer arm against an even split.

Glossary

Usual test
The standard 5% significance test (here Welch's), built for one look at the planned end.
Significant at 5%
If there were no real effect, a difference at least this large would turn up in under 5% of tests.
Sequential rule
A test designed to be checked as often as you like while keeping its false alarms at 5% or less; here an approximation, with its false alarms measured.
Relabelling replay
Real users with the arms thrown away and redrawn at random; any difference found is a false alarm. Also called an A/A test.
Delta method
The interval for a ratio such as revenue per order, computed with the customer as the unit.
CUPED
Adjusting a test's metric for data from before the test, to cut its noise.
Holm correction
A stricter bar when many segments are tested, so chance finds are rarer.
Guardrail
A metric that must not get worse for a change to ship.
Did not hold
An early call that the same test at the last recorded snapshot did not confirm: no longer significant at 5%, or significant the other way.
