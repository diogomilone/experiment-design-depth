Experiment design: which choices change the call

On 2,000 no-effect splits of ad traffic, daily checks at the usual bar raised false alarms in 20.5%; one look, 5%. If you check daily, raise the bar.

A false alarm is a test reporting a difference that is not there, and acting on one ships a change that does nothing. To count them, 14 million visitors from public advertising tests were split into two groups at random 2,000 times, so no real difference existed. Each split was checked daily for 14 days. The usual bar for a difference, 5%, is built for one look at the end; checked daily, it raised a false alarm in 20.5% of splits. A sequential rule, which asks for much stronger evidence in the first days, raised one in 0.6%.

So a team that checks daily should raise the bar early, or decide once at the end. The raised bar can be a sequential rule, or the 5% split evenly across the planned looks: 0.36% at each of 14 daily looks.

Real experiments, where the truth is not known, point the same way. The 381 readings are each one metric of one tested version, from a fashion retailer's 78 experiments. Daily checks at the usual bar made 219 calls to ship or drop a change. Pooled over four metrics, the usual test on each experiment's full data did not confirm 53% of them, against 30% for the sequential rule. That later test shares data with the early call, so it favours any stricter bar.

What to do

Write the stopping rule into the test plan before launch. For daily looks, use a sequential rule or the 5% split evenly across the planned looks; otherwise decide once at the planned end.

For per-order metrics such as revenue per order, count customers, not orders: one customer's orders rise and fall together. Adjusting for what customers did before a test stays a trial, not a rule (design-choices page).

What it is worth

Per 100 readings, the sequential rule left about 24 fewer calls that the full-data test did not confirm, and reached about 10 fewer of its findings. Assume a team runs 100 tests a year, each on one main metric, and that the full-data test is right. If half of those calls and findings are ships worth US$25,000 each, the net is about US$170,000 a year, between US$17,000 and US$1.0 million across the assumed ranges.

How I would put this to work with a team

  1. Week one. The analytics lead writes the stopping rule into each test plan; the product manager signs it before launch.
  2. Week two. A data engineer puts the raised bar on the dashboard.
  3. Weeks three and four. The analyst replays the team's past tests day by day and counts the findings the raised bar misses against one look at the end (here 41 of 105). The product lead decides whether fewer unconfirmed calls are worth those misses.
  4. Every test after that. Per-order metrics use the customer as the unit; segment cuts not named in the plan are leads, not results.

About 1 engineer-week and 3 analyst-weeks, assumed.

The three numbers that matter

  • 20.5% against 5%. False alarms in 2,000 no-effect splits, checked daily at the usual bar against one look at the end.
  • 53% against 30%. Real-test calls the full-data test did not confirm, usual bar against sequential rule, four metrics pooled. A fixed bar as strict, tuned on these tests after the results, gave 31%.
  • 22% against 5%. False alarms on revenue per order in random splits of a real store's customers, counting orders against counting customers.

One test, read out

A public e-mail test: the women's e-mail against none, about 21,300 customers each. Visits rose 4.5 points, a 43% lift (95% interval 3.9 to 5.2 points), and conversion, the guardrail, rose too. The test had 40% of the customers the design's 5% target needed. Read as one call, chosen after the results: ship the women's e-mail.

The main limit

The design made the real-test count the main result instead, since the replay's size is set by its looks. Ten other random seeds gave 20.9% to 22.4%, so 20.5% is the low end, and the days are simulated. The even split was not tested here; it holds false alarms to 5% by arithmetic. Spending plans such as Pocock's are the usual, less strict choice. On the real tests, a fixed bar as strict did about as well, and by metric the sequential rule's share runs from 10% to 53%. A higher bar also reaches fewer findings: 64 of the 105 the full data showed, against 102 for the usual daily check. The money leaves out set-up time and the cost of waiting. The e-mail test is one public test read after its data.

What would change the conclusion

  • If random splits of a team's own traffic put the chosen bar's 95% false-alarm interval above 5% (sequential rule here: 0.3% to 1.0%), decide once at the end.
  • If replaying past tests daily shows the raised bar missing over half of what one final look finds (here 41 of 105), decide once at the end. That threshold was chosen after the results.
